# Genre adapters

Apply one adapter after building the common argument graph. A document can be mixed; use the dominant mode and note the secondary mode.

## Sermon or Bible teaching

Preserve four layers:

1. **Text** — passage, quotation, translation, and immediate context.
2. **Exegesis** — what the speaker says the text means.
3. **Doctrine/theology** — claims generalized beyond the passage.
4. **Application** — what the congregation is asked to believe or practice.

Do not turn a sermon into generic motivational advice. Keep the opening question, the passage’s role, the speaker’s interpretive moves, and the final response. Use the character as a listener, guide, or witness; use diagrams and symbolic objects for sacred figures and events unless the user requests literal depiction.

## Essay

Preserve the author’s voice and ambiguity. Build: observation or problem → thesis → development → counterpoint/turn → implication → resonant close. Do not force a three-point sermon structure onto a reflective essay. Keep one memorable sentence or image per major turn, with the fuller prose in notes.

## Column or opinion piece

Build: hook → concrete event → claim/stance → evidence or example → complication → closing sentence. Make the author’s position visible and distinguish reported facts from opinion. Do not present a provocative framing as neutral fact.

## Lecture, article, or explanatory note

Build: question → concept definitions → mechanism/process → evidence/example → synthesis → practical consequence. Prefer a timeline, process diagram, comparison, or small table when it reduces cognitive load.

## Mixed form

When a source combines story, exposition, and application, label each slide’s role explicitly. Keep story scenes for attention, explanatory diagrams for causal structure, and application slides for audience response; do not make every page perform all three jobs.
