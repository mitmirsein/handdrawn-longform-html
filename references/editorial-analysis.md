# Editorial analysis

The primary job is to recover the author’s reasoning before choosing layouts or images.

## Source profile

Record:

- title, author/speaker, date, language, source path, and genre;
- likely audience and intended response;
- central question and one-sentence thesis;
- major sections and their function in the whole argument;
- emotional register, rhetorical posture, and unresolved ambiguities.

Do not confuse the first heading with the thesis. The thesis must explain what the author wants the audience to understand, believe, reconsider, or do.

## Block classification

For each paragraph or small contiguous group, assign one or more labels:

```text
BACKGROUND       time, place, people, definitions, setup
THESIS           answer or controlling claim
CLAIM            proposition that advances the thesis
EVIDENCE         data, passage, example, observation, authority
QUOTE            words preserved from the source or cited text
INTERPRETATION   author’s reading of evidence
ANALOGY          metaphor or comparison
OBJECTION        question, counterclaim, limitation, doubt
APPLICATION      consequence for the audience
TRANSITION       movement to the next idea
RECAP            intentional repetition or conclusion
```

Keep source line ranges for every important block. Rhetorical questions and repeated phrases are often delivery devices; preserve their force in a headline, callout, or speaker note instead of duplicating the same slide.

## Argument graph

Represent the minimum reasoning chain:

```text
question → thesis → claim → evidence → interpretation → implication/application
                         ↘ objection/counterpoint → response
```

Every non-cover slide should connect to at least one claim or transition in this graph. If a paragraph is interesting but does not advance the graph, move it to speaker notes or omit it.

## Compression rules

- Preserve the thesis, turning points, evidence that changes belief, and final application.
- Merge adjacent paragraphs that perform the same logical job.
- Turn repeated oral prompts (“무엇입니까?”, “기억하셨습니까?”) into one deliberate question or speaker-note cue.
- Never combine two claims merely because they share a keyword.
- Do not use a visual metaphor that changes agency, causality, or moral responsibility.
- Keep the visible takeaway short; move proof, qualifications, and long quotations to notes.

## Quality questions

Before rendering, answer:

1. Can the thesis be stated without reading the whole deck?
2. Does each page change the audience’s understanding or prepare the next page?
3. Is every important conclusion supported by a source block or clearly marked interpretation?
4. Did compression remove a necessary qualification or counterargument?
5. Does the ending resolve the opening question rather than merely repeat the title?
